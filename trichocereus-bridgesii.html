Trichocereus bridgesii

A Bolivian columnar cactus with many distinct cultivated selections. Kew's accepted name for it is Echinopsis lageniformis, though "bridgesii" is the name almost all growers use.

Description

Britton and Rose (1920) described a branching columnar plant with pale-green, slightly glaucous stems, four to eight ribs and a few very unequal yellowish spines.

Stem and ribs

A fast-growing, often shrubby to small-tree-like columnar cactus, usually four to eight ribs (most commonly reported five to seven), pale to mid-green stems carrying a slight blue-grey cast. Mature stems are reported 10–20 cm across, with plants reaching 2–5 m.

Areoles and spines

Areoles sit roughly 2–3 cm apart and carry two to six spines per areole; reported spine length varies widely, from under 1 cm up to roughly 6–7 cm and occasionally to about 10 cm, honey-colored ageing to brown — among the longest-spined of the commonly cultivated Trichocereus/Echinopsis.

Flowers

Large, white, night-opening and fragrant, reported around 15–18 cm long.

Distribution and habitat

Native to Bolivia (POWO); recorded as introduced in the Canary Islands and Spain. A plant encountered in a market outside Bolivia is not, by itself, evidence of a native population there.

Similar species

Long, drooping spines are the character most often cited against pachanoi, which is usually far more sparsely spined, and against peruvianus, whose spines are typically shorter and whose stem color is more consistently blue. Four-ribbed plants are sometimes sold under names implying a fixed form, but sources note that rib count can vary within a single plant over time, so it is not a dependable identifier on its own.

Cultivation

The ranges below are reported starting points drawn from cultivation sources, not a fixed schedule — adjust for your own climate, pot size and season. Do not label every dark lesion "bridgesii black rot" or assume one pathogen: record whether tissue is firm or collapsing, whether damage spreads, and what changed in watering, temperature or handling; see the cactus health guide. Cold tolerance also depends on exposure duration, moisture and prior conditions, not on a single reported figure — protect plants from unfamiliar cold and track your own results.

Cultivars and forms

Two child pages cover named horticultural branches of this species: scopulicola, a distinct grower identity tied to one 1959 Ritter field collection, and bridgesii monstrose (TBM), an irregular growth form. Taxonomy and names

Kew's accepted name is Echinopsis lageniformis (C.F.Först.) H.Friedrich & G.D.Rowley (1974). Trichocereus bridgesii (1920), Trichocereus scopulicola (1966) and Trichocereus crassicostatus (1966) are all heterotypic synonyms — different nomenclatural types folded into one species, not the same plant under three names. The oldest validly published name for this group is Cereus lageniformis C.F.Först. (1861), which is why the accepted epithet is "lageniformis" even though "bridgesii" is the name almost all growers use.

This taxonomic grouping does not establish that a bridgesii cutting and a named scopulicola selection are the same clone. Keep the horticultural distinction visible: record the supplied name and provenance for each plant rather than treating the species-level merger as proof of shared identity.

Conservation and legal status

IUCN Red List: Least Concern, population unknown. Assessed by Lowry, M. (2017, an amended version of a 2013 assessment, originally assessed in 2010); IUCN Red List 2017: e.T152817A121489925.

CITES: the whole family Cactaceae, including this species, is listed on CITES Appendix II; it is not among the small set of cactus taxa singled out for the stricter Appendix I.
