Substrate basics

A cactus mix has one real job: give the roots air, let excess water leave quickly, and dry out in a way you can predict from one watering to the next. Bagged "cactus soil," a homemade blend and a mostly inorganic mix can all do that job if the particle sizes are right — the ingredient list on the bag usually matters less than how coarse and how uniform the particles inside it actually are.

There is no single ratio that is correct for every species, pot and climate. A fine-rooted seedling in a small nursery cell, a mature columnar cactus in a large glazed pot, and a cactus grown outdoors somewhere rainy all want different amounts of drainage material. Treat every recipe on this page, including the well-known "gritty mix," as a starting point you adjust after watching how it actually behaves in your own pots — see Growing cacti well for the wider care routine this fits into.

Recipes

The recipe growers call the "gritty mix" is not a laboratory standard; it is a formula that spread through cactus, succulent and bonsai forums, most often credited to a contributor who posted under the name Al ("tapla"), documented in detail in gardening-forum threads such as one preserved on Houzz. The classic ratio is equal parts by volume of screened calcined clay (kiln-fired clay sold as Turface, Akadama or similar), screened fir or pine bark fines, and crushed granite or grit, each sieved to roughly 1/8–1/4 inch and screened free of dust — so the three ingredients settle together as evenly sized particles instead of the fine material clogging the gaps between the coarse material.

It is one well-documented starting point among several reasonable ones, not the only correct answer. The table below runs from the most involved recipe to the simplest.

RecipeRatioParticle notesA starting point for
Gritty mix (1:1:1)1 part calcined clay : 1 part bark fines : 1 part crushed granite/gritSieved to roughly 1/8–1/4 in; dust screened out of each ingredientGrowers willing to source and screen three separate materials; mixed desert cactus and succulent collections
Pumice-heavy mix1 part bagged potting soil : 2 parts pumicePumice graded roughly 1/8–3/8 inA simpler all-purpose option with only two ingredients to buy
Bagged mix + pumice (beginner)2 parts commercial cactus/succulent mix : 1 part pumicePumice sieved of dust before addingA first mix while you are still learning how fast your pots dry
Seedling / sifted mixStandard cactus mix passed through a 1/8 in screen, used as a thin top layer over a coarser baseFine, uniform, no chunksSeed trays and small seedlings — see Cacti from seed

Whichever recipe you start from, sieve out dust and very fine particles before use. That fine fraction is exactly what fills the air gaps a gritty-style mix is built to keep open.

Comparing ingredients

Every material in a cactus mix is doing one of two jobs: holding open air space and moving water through quickly (the mineral, "grit" side), or holding a little water and some nutrients (the organic side). The table below compares the common options.

IngredientRoleWatch for
PumiceMineral; drainage plus light internal water-holdingPorous and stable; does not float or break down quickly; heavier than perlite so it stays mixed in
PerliteMineral; drainageVery light; sheds water fast but tends to float to the surface after watering and can compact over repeated wet/dry cycles
Calcined clay (Turface/Akadama-type)Mineral; drainage plus some internal water and nutrient holdingKiln-fired clay; quality and durability vary by product, and particles eventually crumble
Lava rock (scoria)Mineral; drainageCoarse and stable; less internal water-holding than pumice
Granite grit / crushed rockMineral; drainage and weightAngular and stable; useful for top-heavy plants in light mixes
Bark fines (fir/pine)Organic; drainage plus slow water and nutrient holdingScreen to match the other particle sizes; breaks down over one to a few years and needs topping up
Coir / peatOrganic; water and nutrient holdingHolds far more water than bark; useful in small amounts for epiphytic cacti, easy to overdo for desert species
SandMineral, but only if coarseFine sand packs into the gaps between larger particles and blocks the air spaces the rest of the mix is built around; only coarse, washed sand belongs in a cactus mix, and it is optional even then

Pumice vs. perlite is the comparison growers ask about most. Both are lightweight, volcanic, mineral amendments that add air space and drainage, but they age differently in a pot. Perlite is cheaper and easier to find, but its low density lets it float upward with repeated watering and it can lose structure over time. Pumice is heavier and denser, stays in place, and keeps its shape for longer, which is why growers who do not repot often tend to prefer it. Neither is "wrong" for a cactus mix; the difference shows up mainly a year or two into the life of a pot, not in how the plant responds on day one.

Testing a mix

Before repotting a whole collection into a new recipe, mix a small trial batch, wet it thoroughly in a spare pot, and watch how long it actually takes to dry. A fast drip out of the drainage holes only shows that the bottom of the pot is not waterlogged; it does not prove that the root zone a couple of inches up has actually dried. Push a finger in, or use a wooden skewer, a day or two after watering and again after a week, and compare pots side by side rather than trusting the recipe on its own. This is the same small-batch, wet-and-watch method described for any cactus mix in Soil.

Repeat the test whenever you change pot size, pot material (unglazed terracotta dries very differently from glazed ceramic or plastic), or move a plant to a different spot — not just the first time you build a recipe.

Watering and feeding

A gritty or pumice-heavy mix dries faster than a mix built mostly from potting soil, simply because it holds less water per unit of volume — that is the intended effect, but it means the same plant in the same pot may need water more often once you switch mixes. Check the drying interval again rather than assuming the old schedule still applies, and reduce watering further as growth slows in cooler, shorter-day conditions. If a plant looks stressed after a mix change, Cactus health covers how to separate a watering problem from root damage or disease rather than guessing from a symptom photo.

A mineral-heavy mix also holds very little in the way of nutrients on its own, so a plant growing in it depends more on what you actively feed it. Published guidance for cacti and related plants generally points toward a diluted, complete liquid feed on a monthly schedule through the growing season, reduced or stopped once growth slows — one published feeding routine for potted cacti, for example, calls for a diluted balanced or high-potassium feed monthly in spring and summer, with feeding stopped later in the season. Treat any specific product, dilution or frequency as a label to read and adjust for your own mix and climate, not a fixed rule that transfers unchanged to every species.

Practice in Gritty Mix

Gritty Mix lets you experiment with substrate choices without risking real plants — swap ingredients, change ratios, and see how a virtual pot responds over time. Its drying rates and growth numbers are built for play, not measured cultivation predictions; use it to build intuition about the trade-offs above, then check anything that actually matters against a real trial batch in a real pot.
