Description

Britton and Rose (1920) described stout, upright or ascending branches, blue-waxed young growth, six to eight rounded ribs and robust brown spines, from material collected near Matucana, Peru, in 1914; mature flowers were not seen for that original description.

Stem and ribs

Erect columnar habit, generally six to eight ribs (occasionally five or nine), with bluish-green stems reported 6–15 cm across, occasionally more.

Areoles and spines

Older areoles can carry up to twenty spines, with three or four longer, more robust central spines reported up to about 5 cm and darker toward the tip; younger or more shaded plants are often described with far fewer, shorter spines. Areoles are spaced more than 2.5 cm apart.

Flowers

Carried mostly near the stem tips, reported up to about 21 cm long, with tepals described as pale yellow or yellowish-green, rarely pink — a departure from the white flowers typical of pachanoi and bridgesii.

Size

Established plants are reported reaching 2.5–5 m.

Distribution and habitat

Native to Bolivia and Peru — western and southern Peru into Bolivia (POWO). Recorded as introduced in the Balearic Islands, the Canary Islands, Colombia and Spain.

Similar species

A blue-frosted stem and yellowish rather than white flower color are the characters most often cited to separate peruvianus-labeled plants from pachanoi and bridgesii, though flower color can be hard to confirm without seeing a plant bloom, and spine count is reported to increase substantially with age and light exposure — a young, shaded plant can look nearly spineless. Because Kew's current treatment folds peruvianus, Trichocereus macrogonus and several other historical names into one species, matching a garden label to a precise wild population is often not possible from morphology alone.

Cultivation

The ranges below are reported starting points drawn from cultivation sources, not a fixed schedule — adjust for your own climate, pot size and season.

Taxonomy and names

Kew's accepted name for this plant is Echinopsis macrogona (Salm-Dyck) H.Friedrich & G.D.Rowley (1974), native to Bolivia and Peru. Trichocereus peruvianus Britton & Rose (1920) and Echinopsis peruviana (Britton & Rose) H.Friedrich & G.D.Rowley (1974) are both heterotypic synonyms of it; Trichocereus macrogonus (Salm-Dyck) Riccob. (1909) is the homotypic synonym of the same accepted name (sharing a type with Cereus macrogonus Salm-Dyck, 1850) and is itself the accepted name under Albesiano's 2012 "alternative taxonomy" cited by Kew. All of these names — peruvianus, macrogonus, macrogona and peruviana — converge on one Kew-recognized species.

Despite that taxonomic merger, growers keep "peruvianus" and "macrogonus" as distinct horticultural identities, because the two names circulate in cultivation with different source populations, named selections and seed-lot histories, even though Kew currently treats them as one species. A taxonomic merger does not establish which seed population or named clone a particular nursery plant descends from — see the GeeBee reference and preserve the received name and its later determination separately. Joël Lodé's competing classification keeps the genus Trichocereus separate from Echinopsis and, in 2024, published Trichocereus macrogonus subsp. peruvianus rather than folding this plant into Echinopsis macrogona.

Conservation and legal status

Not evaluated by the IUCN Red List under the name Echinopsis macrogona; this also covers peruvianus and peruviana by synonymy.

CITES: the whole family Cactaceae, including this species, is listed on CITES Appendix II; it is not among the small set of cactus taxa singled out for the stricter Appendix I.
