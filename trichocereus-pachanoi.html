Trichocereus pachanoi

San Pedro cactus. Kew's accepted name for this species is Echinopsis pachanoi; "Trichocereus pachanoi" is the name most growers search and sell it under.

Description

Britton and Rose (1920) described an upright, branching cactus with six to eight broad ribs, often few or no spines, and very large, fragrant white flowers opening at night; young stems were slightly glaucous, becoming darker green with age. Their name honors Abelardo Pachano.

Stem and ribs

Upright, freely branching columnar habit, usually with six to eight ribs. Stems are reported 6–15 cm across, light to dark green and sometimes lightly glaucous.

Areoles and spines

Areoles sit roughly 2 cm apart along each rib and typically carry up to seven spines, yellow ageing to brown, most under 2 cm long. Cultivated plants are frequently described as nearly spineless.

Flowers

Large, white and strongly fragrant, opening at night, reported 19–24 cm long with a bloom diameter up to 20 cm, lasting about two days before closing.

Size

Established plants are reported reaching 3–6 m, occasionally taller, branching mainly from the base.

Distribution and habitat

Native to Ecuador and Peru, from southern Ecuador into Peru (POWO). Recorded as introduced elsewhere, including Bolivia, the Canary Islands, central Chile, Colombia and Spain — those introduced records are distinct from the native range.

Similar species

Spine length and stem color are the field cues cited most often, though neither settles an identification alone. Compare against peruvianus, usually reported with a more pronounced blue-frosted stem and longer spines, and bridgesii, whose spines are consistently reported much longer and more numerous per areole. Rib counts overlap across all three, so they are not a reliable sole distinguisher.

Cultivars and forms

A botanical synonym names the whole species; a clone or cultivar name names one individual plant and its vegetative descendants. See PC / "pachanot" for this species' best-documented horticultural clone names. Grower-named clones include T. pachanoi 'Juul's Giant' (TJG) and T. pachanoi 'Torres & Torres'.

Taxonomy and names

Trichocereus pachanoi, Echinopsis pachanoi and Trichocereus macrogonus var. pachanoi are the same nomenclatural type under three different combinations: Trichocereus pachanoi Britton & Rose (1920) is the original combination, Echinopsis pachanoi (Britton & Rose) H.Friedrich & G.D.Rowley (1974) is Kew's current accepted name, and the var. pachanoi combination is a later, different-rank treatment of the same type. Sharing a type ("homotypic") means the names are nomenclaturally linked, not that every living plant sold under any of them is genetically identical — keep the botanical name and an individual plant's provenance in separate fields. See the lineage guide.

Kew's accepted name is an attributed taxonomic treatment, not a universal name every author must follow. Joël Lodé's competing classification keeps the genus Trichocereus separate from Echinopsis and treats this plant as Trichocereus macrogonus subsp. pachanoi rather than folding it into Echinopsis pachanoi. Whether "pachanoi" is best treated as its own species or as part of a broader Trichocereus/Echinopsis macrogonus complex remains an open naming question among sources. Growers overwhelmingly continue to search for and sell this plant as "Trichocereus pachanoi," which is why that name is kept as this page's heading; see Trichocereus / Echinopsis for the wider genus-level picture.

Conservation and legal status

IUCN Red List: Least Concern, population stable. Assessed by Ostolaza, C., Cáceres, F. & Roque, J. (2017, an amended version of a 2013 assessment, originally assessed in 2011); IUCN Red List 2017: e.T152445A121474583.

CITES: the whole family Cactaceae, including this species, is listed on CITES Appendix II; pachanoi is not among the small set of cactus taxa singled out for the stricter Appendix I.
